How we measure it
  1. We add up the cash US companies paid their shareholders over the past 12 months. That is dividends, plus what companies spent buying back their own shares, minus what they raised selling new shares. The figures for buybacks and new shares leave out banks and insurers.
  2. We then ask how much money, lent at —% a year, would earn that much in interest. We call that sum the payout value.
  3. The rate is what highly rated companies pay on 20-year bonds, which are loans from investors. The US Treasury publishes it monthly, and we use each month’s rate from the start of the next month.
  4. We compare the payout value with the value of all US company stock, from the Federal Reserve. Speculation is the stock value minus the payout value.
  5. We use each Federal Reserve figure only from 75 days after its quarter ends, about when it comes out. So each past reading uses only numbers that were public at the time. Between releases, daily moves in the S&P 500 stock index update the stock value. Dividends from a fund that tracks that index update the dividend total.

We wanted a rate set by the market, not a fixed number, that we are free to publish. This one moves almost exactly like a Moody’s rate on company bonds, which we used before. We can’t publish Moody’s rate, but we can publish the Treasury’s.

The idea comes from Karl Marx. He called stocks “fictitious capital,” because they are claims on future income. He wrote that their market value “is in part speculative.” It is determined “not only by the actual income, but also by the anticipated income.” (Capital, Volume III, chapter 29.)

Our data comes from the US Federal Reserve, the US Treasury and Yahoo Finance. The data file and our code are linked at the bottom of this page.

What it can’t tell you
  • It can’t tell you when stock prices will fall, or by how much. The longest earlier stretch with speculation above half the value ran from —, to —.
  • Some speculation is normal. Prices usually include what buyers expect companies to pay in the future.
  • When companies raise more by selling new shares than they spend buying back shares, payouts shrink. Speculation then looks bigger.
  • The answer depends on how we count buybacks. If we average them over 3 years instead of 1, speculation is —% of the value today.
  • The interest rate changes every month. A higher rate means a lower payout value and more speculation, even if nothing else changes.
  • The Federal Reserve updates its past numbers every 3 months. When it does, our past numbers change too.
